Where should the agents live —
and why should they exist?

Taylor Silva · Staff Product Designer, AI UX · June 2026 · companion to the Smart Window Motion System

Try them, don't read them: the live prototype runs all six concepts on one engine — type your own asks, answer the setup questions with your own details, edit rules on the cards, skip days, and get the alerts. Picking a concept in the top bar restarts the experience from the very beginning, so every concept is felt as a cold start. Storyboards below are for decks.

The problem is two-layered

The 1:1 surfaced two questions that get conflated but must be answered separately:

  1. The surface layer — where do agents and their outputs live so they're discoverable, retrievable, and don't sprawl? Candidate homes from the meeting: slash panel, sidebar, FAB widget, chat history, new tab.
  2. The value layer — what do these agents do that a classic browser window can't, and how do Monitor / Scout / Research stay distinct from each other and from the general assistant?

The surface question can't be answered before the value question, because what an agent produces determines where it must live. The meeting's sharpest insight was exactly this: reports and live artifacts are different objects. A live watch has state ("checked 14m ago, still $82"); a report is a finished document you'll want in two weeks. Surfaces that treat them identically — pure chat history — fail one or the other.

Working principle from the meeting, kept: redundancy is good. An object can live in its chat thread and have a dedicated retrievable home. The Claude-artifacts anecdote (user couldn't find the artifact because the icon was hidden) is a documented failure mode — and Anthropic's own answer was a dedicated artifacts space, while OpenAI retrofitted a fullscreen report viewer in Feb 2026 because Deep Research reports were getting buried in chat history. The industry has already paid for this lesson twice.

Resolving the agent taxonomy first

Three agents are on the table, per the meeting:

AgentWhat it doesTime shapeOutput object
MonitorWatches a page or group of pages for a condition — price hits $75, restock, a refundable fare under $850 appears. Minimal input, never a form.Ongoing — quiet until the condition is trueLive artifact — has state, breathes, ends in an alert
ScoutHunts the best deal for something you've already identified: sweeps retailers, compares effective prices (shipping, bundles, member pricing), finds and verifies promo codes against the listed price — reported, never applied. The Honey use case, minus Honey's baggage.Immediate sweep, then optionally keeps hunting in the backgroundScout result now + a live artifact if it keeps hunting
Research"I don't know what I want yet — compare options and tell me what you'd do."One run, minutesReport — finished document, kept

The meeting flagged that Monitor vs. Scout is blurry from the user's side. The resolution here: they share one surface object — a Watch on the Den — but Scout stays a named behavior the user actually meets. "Find me the best deal on this" runs Scout immediately and shows its sweep; "Scout found SAVE10 — verified, not applied" is how deal results are attributed in alerts and on shelf cards. The real user-facing difference is time shape: Scout answers now, Monitor answers when the world changes — and a Scout that keeps hunting simply becomes a Watch. Shipping three top-level entry points would be a vocabulary tax (Arc died of novelty tax); shipping Scout as a visible, named behavior inside two verbs is not.

This also answers the Research-overlap worry: Research is distinguished from the general assistant by its output contract — it always produces a kept, citable, retrievable report, not an answer that evaporates upward in a scroll. If it doesn't deserve a report, it's just chat, and that's fine.

Why an LLM at all — the bar each concept must clear

The open question from the meeting (for Tyler): a classic browser can scan pages, so why does this need an LLM? The answer is concrete, and the prototypes each demonstrate it. A classic watcher (CamelCamelCamel, Distill.io, Honey) binds to pixels and selectors. The LLM binds to meaning. That difference shows up in four places:

  • Semantic conditions. "Refundable, under $850, weekday departures" — three soft constraints judged across 47 results. No DOM-diff can know "refundable" from "basic economy." Try it in the live prototype — type a rule like that and watch it parse.
  • Resilience. Site redesigns break selector-based watchers silently. An LLM reading the page like a person survives the redesign — and knows a "sale" banner, member pricing, or a bundle counts as a price change.
  • Synthesis across pages. "Watch these 3 retailers as a group, alert me once with the best" is one judgment over multiple sources — grouped monitoring with minimal input, the exact requirement from the meeting.
  • The notification layer. A classic watcher says "page changed." The agent says "$834 refundable, Tuesday, $16 under your cap — fares at this level lasted ~2 days last month." The meeting's hunch that the value is in the experience/notification layer is right, but it's downstream of the capability: judgment is what makes the notification worth sending.

The honest counterpoint: if an agent's alerts aren't better than Honey's, users will correctly treat it as a worse Honey. The quality bar lives in the notification: every alert must carry a sentence of judgment a scanner couldn't write. That's a content/eval problem as much as a design one — flagging for KD's content questions.

Six concepts, compared

All six share the resolved taxonomy (Monitor + Scout surfacing as Watch, plus Research), the same scenario set, and the Smart Window motion system. Every concept shares the same five triggers — slash command, suggestion chips, just asking, Smart Window offering mid-conversation, and the URL-bar pill. They differ only on the question that matters: where the watch lives and how it reaches you. A, B, and C came first; the stress-test pass produced D; E moves the destination into Firefox View; F combines the Den with a Dens section in Firefox View and a live bar pill. Nothing is final this round, but F — Den everywhere — is the leading direction: it keeps the den in the sidebar panel, in Firefox View, and on the page itself.

Concept A — All in the chat

The strongest version of "chat history is enough." Watches start by typing in the chat; confirmation is an in-thread card you edit by replying. Two small additions carry everything: a pinned line at the top of the panel showing what's running, and — since chat history actually lives in Firefox View → Chats — the watch appears there as a child row under the chat that started it, with its live dot and rule. Clicking either takes you back into the conversation.

Pros

  • Zero new surfaces — the conversation, the pinned chip, and Firefox View's existing history carry everything
  • The watch is a live card in the thread: sparkline, stats, Edit via question card, Pause, Delete
  • The pinned chip expands to full detail in place, so chat continues underneath
  • Cheapest build and fastest to ship

Cons

  • The thread is still the only record — long chats bury the card
  • The pin row stops scaling past a few watches
  • Nothing reaches you on the page you're shopping on

Concept B — A separate agents page

The "no constraints" direction, built honestly rather than as a strawman: it's genuinely the best home agents could have, with structural discoverability (the new tab is visited daily), full transparency via the activity feed, and obvious slots for Routine digests and someday Errands.

Pros

  • The strongest management story: every agent a card, reports, and a log of every check
  • The living pin keeps the watch one tap away while you chat
  • The new-tab summary card makes the hub discoverable daily
  • Setup questions make slash-created watches as good as contextual ones

Cons

  • The most expensive single surface to build, localize, and maintain
  • A destination one click away from where intent happens
  • New-tab real estate is the most contested in the browser

Concept C — A saved list in the panel

The synthesis. Entry: a quiet, dismissible chip in the URL bar when context warrants ("I can watch these fares") — pull-first proactivity. Confirmation: a plan-before-run card, read-only promise explicit. Home: a Den segment inside the existing Smart Window panel holding exactly two object types — Live and Reports. Reports open as canvas in the content area with the linked chat rail. Everything is redundantly reachable from its original thread.

Pros

  • Each chat keeps its own Den — nothing from other conversations bleeds in
  • Never empty: suggestions fill it before you've made anything
  • Zero-typing start; Change-the-rule is a pick-save-go question card
  • Den items are live: rule chips, sparkline, stats, Edit / Pause / Delete
  • Alerts fire in the system corner — they find you off-page

Cons

  • Only works inside the Smart Window panel — Classic windows see nothing
  • No single view across chats (that's what E and F add)
  • Panel real estate is shared with the conversation itself

Concept D — In the chat + the address bar

The lightest concept that still never loses you. Creation: a prefilled, editable card in the chat — click the underlined parts to change the rule, toggle sites, start. The record stays in the chat; find it later by scrolling, which is the honest trade. Live state comes to you instead: an always-there eye in the address bar on watched pages (hover it for the status card — price, trend, rule, pause), and when the condition hits, a floating notification card with the judgment and the actions that matter (Open Kayak · Snooze · Stop). The page content is never modified; everything rides chrome Firefox owns.

Pros

  • Works in Classic windows — dissolves the mode-scoping break (agents are user-level, Smart Window is opt-in)
  • The waking tab bridges the notification-permission cliff — a fallback channel that can't be denied
  • Reports as real pages: printable, bookmarkable, shareable URLs
  • Un-copyable: competitors bolt panels beside the web; only the rendering engine's owner annotates into it
  • Footprint over C is two native browser primitives, not new surfaces

Cons

  • Never touches page content — the eye rides the address bar, a surface Firefox already owns (same pattern as the bookmark star)
  • No roster: five watches means nothing to manage them with — that's C's Den or F's page
  • The chat record gets buried as the conversation grows — by design, but real
  • Hover cards and floating notifications need careful frequency discipline to stay quiet

Concept E — Dens in Firefox View

Concept B's management destination without B's biggest costs. A Dens section in Firefox View, beside Chats — the page where chat history already lives. Each watch is a card: the rule in plain words, a dot per check, last-check time, cadence, site count, and direct actions (Check now · Pause · Open page · Delete). When a condition hits, the alert lands as a strip on the card with the judgment and one action. Reports file beneath, opening with their chat attached. Creation happens in the chat: the offer chip (or just asking) opens the sidebar with the prefilled, editable card inline. No address-bar indicator — Firefox View is the one home.

Pros

  • Full management surface at a fraction of B's cost — Firefox View already ships
  • No day-one empty dashboard: it's one rail item until you have dens
  • Sits beside Chats — history and agents in one familiar place
  • No new entry point: the chip and the chat are the doors

Cons

  • Firefox View is low-traffic for many users — discoverability depends on the rail badge and notifications
  • Still a destination you go to; nothing reaches you on the page itself — that trade is deliberate (D covers the opposite)
  • Firefox View real estate is owned by another team — coordination cost

Concept F — Den everywhere (leading direction · the combination)

The Den treated as the one true object, projected to three places that stay in sync. In the panel: every chat keeps its own Den behind a Chat | Den toggle at the top of the sidebar, exactly as in C. In Firefox View: a Dens section beside Chats — each chat's den appears with all its children inside: monitors with their check history and actions, reports, and generated tabs. In the bar: on a watched page, a labeled pill (Watching · $84) that updates live as checks come in. After creation, the chat says it in a sentence — "you can also find it in your Dens" — with the link.

Pros

  • Best discoverability and retrieval of the set — intent-time (pill), in-context (chat den), and destination (Dens page) all covered
  • One mental model: the den; everything else is a projection of it
  • Dens hold mixed children — monitors, reports, gen tabs — grouped by the chat that made them
  • Grows by adding child types, never surfaces

Cons

  • The largest build of any concept: three surfaces that must never disagree
  • The largest near-term scope — the natural path is shipping C and growing into F
  • Firefox View real estate needs another team's buy-in
  • Naming risk: "den" must read instantly or the whole model wobbles

The long-term synthesis, revisited — how Den everywhere ships gradually

Earlier this round we parked an "everything, added gradually" idea. With F as the leading direction it comes back as F's rollout plan: each projection of the den appears only once there's something to put on it, so no surface ever ships empty and F's build cost spreads across quarters instead of landing at once.

StageWhat appearsBorrowed fromWhen it appears
1 · InvocationSlash, suggestion chips, asking, mid-chat offers, the URL-bar offer pill — plus the question cards for missing detailsADay one, in every smartbar
2 · The den in the panelThe Chat | Den toggle at the top of the sidebar; suggestions fill it before anything startsCFirst chat that could make something

Why stage it this way

  • No empty states anywhere — a surface can't exist without content, by construction
  • One object model (the den) keeps every projection in sync at the data layer
  • F's biggest con — the largest build of the set — becomes a sequence of small ships
  • Each stage is a complete, testable experience on its own (stage 2 is C; stages 2–3 are C+D)

What it costs

  • Still the most total surface to design, build, and localize — multi-quarter
  • Progressive unlock logic is its own feature to test and explain
  • Users at different stages see different browsers ("why don't I have that page?")
  • Stage 4 needs Firefox View real estate — another team's buy-in

Decision matrix

Eight criteria, scored 1–5, organized per concept so each one reads as a profile. Colors match the cards, storyboards, and live prototype.

F is the leading direction. It tops the experience criteria by combining C's den (a place you visit), E's Dens page in Firefox View, and D's live presence on the page (the bar pill) — paying for it with the largest build cost of the set, which is why "Time to ship" is its only weak row. The open questions, answered as designed

Where do the agents live?
Six answers, prototyped: the chat (A), an Agent Hub off the new tab (B), a per-chat Den in the panel (C), chat + the address bar (D), a Dens section in Firefox View (E), or all of those projections of one den (F). Nothing is locked, but F — Den everywhere — is the leading direction; C and D are its strongest building blocks and could ship first on the way there.
How do you start one?
Identically everywhere: slash command, suggestion chips, just asking, Smart Window offering mid-conversation, or the URL-bar pill. Missing details get the little question cards (numbered options, Something else, Skip); context-rich triggers skip most of them.
What's the difference between Monitor, Scout, and Research?
Time shape. Monitor watches a page until a condition is true. Scout sweeps retailers right now — verified codes, effective prices — and can hand off to a Monitor. Research runs once and keeps a report. Scout and Monitor share one surface object (a watch); Scout stays a named behavior in results.
What is a den, exactly?
A per-chat container. Whatever a conversation creates — monitors, reports, gen tabs — are children of that chat's den. The Den tab (C/F) shows this chat's; Firefox View → Dens (E/F) shows every chat's, grouped.
What does the watch look like once it's running?
A live component, everywhere it appears: rule chips, a sparkline against the target, running stats (now / low / checks), and Edit (question card) / Pause / Delete. The page-open action only appears when you're not already on that page.
Where do alerts land?
The top-right system notification corner, in every concept — never assume the user is on the watched page. And whatever is pinned or live updates with the same information at the same moment: A/B's pinned chip flips to the hit state, D upgrades the alert to a floating action card (Open the site · Snooze · Stop), E/F land it as a strip on the den card, and F's bar pill turns to Hit $607.
Why does this need an LLM?
Semantic rules ("refundable, weekday, under $850") judged across results, resilience to page redesigns, grouped judgment across sites, and alerts that carry a sentence of reasoning instead of "page changed."
Where can I try them?
The live prototype — one engine, all six concepts, every trigger. Picking a concept in the top bar restarts the experience from the very beginning, so each one is felt as a cold start. The per-concept storyboards are scripted versions for decks. (The earlier comparison lab covered only A–D in outdated forms and has been retired.)
What does v1 not promise?
Anything past the read-only line: codes are verified against listed prices but never applied; nothing is booked, clicked, or filled. Every check is logged.
