Where should the agents live —
and why should they exist?

Four end-to-end concepts for the Q3 agent release (Monitor, Scout, Research), prototyped in full against the Smart Window design language, weighed against ten competitor products, and resolved into one recommendation — plus a working, unscripted experience prototype built against the real aiwindow code.

Taylor Silva · Staff Product Designer, AI UX · June 2026 · companion to the Smart Window Motion System

Want to feel it instead of read it? Open the experience — the full journey as a real, unscripted product: built against the actual browser/components/aiwindow code from Firefox-AI/firefox-prototypes (fullpage Smart Window, real smartbar gradient, smartwindow-prompts follow-up chips that replace themselves as you act). Entry → agent working → saved to the Shelf and the page itself → retrieved via notification, shelf, or report. For comparing surface options A/B/C/C+D side-by-side, use the concept lab.

The problem is two-layered

The 1:1 surfaced two questions that get conflated but must be answered separately:

  1. The surface layer — where do agents and their outputs live so they're discoverable, retrievable, and don't sprawl? Candidate homes from the meeting: slash panel, sidebar, FAB widget, chat history, new tab.
  2. The value layer — what do these agents do that a classic browser window can't, and how do Monitor / Scout / Research stay distinct from each other and from the general assistant?

The surface question can't be answered before the value question, because what an agent produces determines where it must live. The meeting's sharpest insight was exactly this: reports and live artifacts are different objects. A live watch has state ("checked 14m ago, still $82"); a report is a finished document you'll want in two weeks. Surfaces that treat them identically — pure chat history — fail one or the other.

Working principle from the meeting, kept: redundancy is good. An object can live in its chat thread and have a dedicated retrievable home. The Claude-artifacts anecdote (user couldn't find the artifact because the icon was hidden) is a documented failure mode — and Anthropic's own answer was a dedicated artifacts space, while OpenAI retrofitted a fullscreen report viewer in Feb 2026 because Deep Research reports were getting buried in chat history. The industry has already paid for this lesson twice.

Resolving the agent taxonomy first

The meeting flagged that Monitor vs. Scout is blurry from the user's side — possibly the same behavior with different backends. I'd go further: shipping three named agents is a vocabulary tax the user shouldn't pay. (Arc died of novelty tax; Dia's whole design strategy is hiding new concepts inside familiar UI.) The user-facing model in all three concepts is two verbs:

User-facing verbBackendWhat it isOutput object
WatchMonitor + Scout"Tell me when something I care about becomes true" — price hits $75, restock, working promo code, refundable fare under $850. Scout is a Watch whose condition is "a better deal exists."Live artifact — has state, breathes, ends
ResearchResearch"I don't know what I want yet — compare options and tell me what you'd do."Report — finished document, kept

This collapses the Monitor/Scout ambiguity (it becomes an implementation detail, exactly as the meeting suspected), and it answers the Research-overlap worry: Research is distinguished from the general assistant by its output contract — it always produces a kept, citable, retrievable report, not an answer that evaporates upward in a scroll. If it doesn't deserve a report, it's just chat, and that's fine.

Why an LLM at all — the bar each concept must clear

The open question from the meeting (for Tyler): a classic browser can scan pages, so why does this need an LLM? The answer is concrete, and the prototypes each demonstrate it. A classic watcher (CamelCamelCamel, Distill.io, Honey) binds to pixels and selectors. The LLM binds to meaning. That difference shows up in four places:

  • Semantic conditions. "Refundable, under $850, weekday departures" — three soft constraints judged across 47 results. No DOM-diff can know "refundable" from "basic economy." This is Concept C, step 3.
  • Resilience. Site redesigns break selector-based watchers silently. An LLM reading the page like a person survives the redesign — and knows a "sale" banner, member pricing, or a bundle counts as a price change.
  • Synthesis across pages. "Watch these 3 retailers as a group, alert me once with the best" is one judgment over multiple sources — grouped monitoring with minimal input, the exact requirement from the meeting.
  • The notification layer. A classic watcher says "page changed." The agent says "$834 refundable, Tuesday, $16 under your cap — fares at this level lasted ~2 days last month." The meeting's hunch that the value is in the experience/notification layer is right, but it's downstream of the capability: judgment is what makes the notification worth sending.

The honest counterpoint: if an agent's alerts aren't better than Honey's, users will correctly treat it as a worse Honey. The quality bar lives in the notification: every alert must carry a sentence of judgment a scanner couldn't write. That's a content/eval problem as much as a design one — flagging for KD's content questions.

Concept A — Threadline

The strongest version of "chat history is enough." Monitor and Scout collapse into /watch; confirmation is an in-thread card edited by replying; the one concession to persistence is a pinned strip of live agents atop the panel. Delivery deep-links into the originating thread.

Pros

  • Near-zero maintenance cost; no new surface to localize, test, or sunset
  • Fastest to ship — pressure-relief for the scoping vacuum in the Discover sprint
  • No novelty tax; the composer is already understood
  • Honest fit with "avoid new surfaces without clear justification"

Cons

  • Retrieval re-runs the Claude-artifacts failure: reports buried in history behind a filter users must remember exists
  • Live state invisible when the panel is closed
  • Pinned strip stops scaling past ~3 concurrent watches
  • No natural home for contextual offers or future habit-learned agents — the roadmap hits a wall

Concept B — Mission Control

The "no constraints" direction, built honestly rather than as a strawman: it's genuinely the best home agents could have, with structural discoverability (the new tab is visited daily), full transparency via the activity feed, and obvious slots for Routine digests and someday Errands.

Pros

  • Best-in-class discoverability and retrieval; nothing is ever buried
  • Activity feed = radical transparency, the strongest trust story
  • Scales indefinitely — proactive/habit agents have an obvious home
  • Matches the proven "manage in a list" pattern (ChatGPT Tasks, Comet dashboard)

Cons

  • Maximum maintenance cost: a new about: page, tile system, feed, gallery, canvas — designed, built, localized, maintained
  • One click away from where intent happens; agents feel like a separate app
  • Empty-state problem: a dashboard with zero agents is dead weight for every new user
  • Echoes the Edge mode-ification failure — heavyweight AI real estate before usage justifies it

Concept C — The Shelf

The synthesis. Entry: a quiet, dismissible chip in the URL bar when context warrants ("I can watch these fares") — pull-first proactivity. Confirmation: a plan-before-run card, read-only promise explicit. Home: a Shelf segment inside the existing Smart Window panel holding exactly two object types — Live and Reports. Reports open as canvas in the content area with the linked chat rail. Everything is redundantly reachable from its original thread.

Pros

  • Discoverability without sprawl: extends the panel users already open, no new window-level surface
  • Honors the reports-vs-live distinction structurally (two typed sections)
  • Offer chip puts invocation at the moment of intent — the meeting's flights scenario, shipped quietly
  • Scales by adding object types, not surfaces; "View all" becomes Mission Control when usage demands it
  • Plan-before-run + activity log + pause-all = the Firefox restraint story made visible

Cons

  • Panel real estate is contested; Chat vs. Shelf segmentation must stay ruthlessly simple
  • Offer-chip frequency capping is a real tuning problem (too chatty = Edge backlash; too quiet = never found)
  • More design surface than A: segment, object cards, canvas template, chip behavior
  • Canvas-in-content-area needs a story for "I closed the tab" (answer: it's on the Shelf)

Concept C+D — Waypoints (final)

The red-team amendment to C, and the shipped recommendation. A watch is one user-level object with three projections: a waypoint (Tab Context Badge pinned to the watched price, with sparkline popover, rule, and trust-tier badges), a sleeping tab (🌙 hibernating in the strip, waking with a badge when the condition hits), and the Shelf card (the index — full state, rule editing, pause-all; reports live here and open as real pages with linked chat). Edit the object anywhere and every projection updates. Built entirely from Nova Smart Window Library components — Tab Context Badge, Suggestion Chips, Smart Input, trust-tier cascade.

Pros

  • Works in Classic windows — dissolves the mode-scoping break (agents are user-level, Smart Window is opt-in)
  • The waking tab bridges the notification-permission cliff — a fallback channel that can't be denied
  • Reports as real pages: printable, bookmarkable, shareable URLs
  • Un-copyable: competitors bolt panels beside the web; only the rendering engine's owner annotates into it
  • Footprint over C is two native browser primitives, not new surfaces

Cons

  • In-content annotation walks near the line Firefox historically refused to cross (modifying pages) — needs an AnonymousContent overlay, not DOM injection
  • Waypoints only visible on revisit; no ambient state without the Shelf
  • Sleeping tabs are device-local; cross-device story still runs through the Shelf
  • Three projections of one object = state-sync surface area to test

Concept D — Waypoints: the assumption A, B, and C all shared

All three concepts assume agents live in browser chrome. The fourth answer: they live on the web itself. A watch is pinned to the thing being watched — the price element carries a Tab Context Badge with live state on revisit; the fare search hibernates as a sleeping tab that wakes when its condition hits; reports are real pages with URLs. Chrome keeps only a thin index (the Shelf). It works in Classic windows — dissolving the mode-scoping break — and it's the one design no competitor can copy: ChatGPT, Comet, and Gemini bolt panels beside the web; only the rendering engine's owner can annotate into it.

D alone fails Research retrieval and ambient state, so it's not a replacement — it's the missing limb. The final design is C amended with D's manifestation layer, prototyped in full with Nova Smart Window Library components: Concept C+D — Waypoints →

Final recommendation

Ship Concept C+D — the Shelf as index, watches manifested as waypoints and sleeping tabs on the web itself — with A's slash invocation inside it and B as the documented growth path.

The amendment came from trying to break C: the waking tab bridges the notification-permission cliff, the waypoint works in Classic windows (dissolving the mode-scoping ceiling), and reports-as-real-pages fixes canvas shareability. The object model is the architecture: one user-level object, three projections (waypoint · sleeping tab · Shelf card) — new agent types add object types, never surfaces. Everything below about C's rationale stands, strengthened.

The Shelf is the only option that satisfies all three of the meeting's governing constraints at once: reports get a dedicated retrievable home (typed Reports section → canvas → linked chat, exactly the model proposed in the 1:1), no new window-level surface is created (it's a segment inside the panel Smart Window already owns — the justification bar for new surfaces stays unbreached), and the architecture points where the product is heading (object types absorb Routine digests and eventually Errands; "View all" graduates into Mission Control when — and only when — usage proves the need).

It is also the only concept that puts invocation at the moment of intent. The competitive record is unambiguous: sidebars won, floating widgets lost, push-proactivity burned Microsoft, and both Anthropic and OpenAI had to bolt on dedicated artifact homes after burying outputs in chat. C is the design those ten products' scar tissue points to.

Sequencing: Sprint 1 — Shelf segment + Watch objects + OS notifications (Monitor/Scout value loop complete). Sprint 2 — Report objects + canvas + linked chat (Research loop complete). Sprint 3 — offer chip, shipped dark behind a frequency cap and tuned. The slash panel (A) ships in Sprint 1 for free since it's composer-only. B is explicitly not built until shelf objects per active user crosses a threshold that makes a dashboard earn its rent.

Why not A, in one line: it re-runs the exact discoverability failure (hidden artifacts) that the meeting itself used as evidence — we'd be shipping the anecdote. Why not B, in one line: it spends the team's scarcest resource (a new permanent surface) before a single user has proven they'll keep three agents alive at once.

The meeting's open questions, answered

Where should these agents live?
In the Smart Window panel the user already has: a second segment ("Shelf") holding typed objects, with a quiet offer chip in the URL bar as the contextual entry. No FAB (market abandoned it), no new tab destination yet (premature), no new mode (Edge's lesson).
Do they need a dedicated surface, or is chat history enough?
Chat history alone is not enough — two competitors already paid to learn that. But "dedicated surface" ≠ "new surface": a segment inside the existing panel is dedicated without being new.
Should reports and live artifacts be treated as different things?
Yes — structurally. They're different object types with different lifecycles (state-and-ends vs. finished-and-kept), different sections, different motion (Breathe vs. settled), different retrieval promises.
Are Monitor and Scout actually distinct?
Not to users. One verb — Watch — with the backend choosing scan vs. deal-hunt per the stated condition. Scout is a Watch whose condition is "a better deal exists." The distinction survives as implementation, dies as vocabulary.
Does Research overlap too much with the assistant?
Only if it's defined by effort. Define it by output contract: Research always yields a kept report on the Shelf with linked chat. No report needed → it was just chat → no overlap anxiety.
How little input can Monitor require?
One click from the offer chip (page context pre-filled, plan-before-run shown), or one sentence from the composer. Refinement is conversational, never a form. Grouped targets are one sentence: "also watch Best Buy and the manufacturer."
Should agents be offered contextually?
Yes, but pull-first: a dismissible chip, one offer per context, silent when ignored, frequency-capped. Habit-learned suggestions (weekly events digest) come later and live as Shelf offers, never notifications.
Why LLM over a classic window?
Semantic conditions, redesign resilience, cross-page synthesis, and judgment-bearing notifications — see the value-layer section. This is the answer to take to Tyler, with Concept C step 3 as the demo.
What should v1 not promise, given shopping blockers?
Anything past the read-only line. Payment processors, IAP rules, and cart/auth state (the WhatsApp US-fallback precedent) make end-to-end checkout a non-promise. The Shelf's object model leaves an "Errand" type parked for when that changes — visible in Concept B's gallery as a dashed future slot.
